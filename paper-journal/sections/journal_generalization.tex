\section{Generalization Analysis: Windowed Attention as Task-Dependent Regularizer}
\label{sec:generalization}

Section~\ref{sec:obs2} showed that ApproxDA-TransUNet consistently improves over DA-TransUNet across all three benchmarks with appropriate window selection. This section investigates a complementary question: does the locality prior introduced by windowed attention act as an implicit regularizer, and is its effect task-dependent?

\subsection{Optimization Volatility}

We define \emph{val DSC volatility} as the standard deviation of validation DSC measured at 15-epoch intervals over the final 30\% of training (epochs~210--300, 7 evaluation points). Low volatility indicates stable convergence; high volatility indicates oscillatory optimization near the end of training. Table~\ref{tab:volatility} reports volatility alongside best validation DSC, test DSC, and the val--test generalization gap for all six model--dataset combinations.

\subsection{CS-Dependent Volatility Crossover}

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_f1_generalization_gap.pdf}
  \caption{Validation DSC trajectories (left column) and training loss trajectories (right column) for DA-TransUNet vs.\ ApproxDA-TransUNet on Synapse (top, high GCS), Kvasir (middle, low GCS), and ISIC (bottom, low GCS). ApproxDA achieves 9--18$\times$ lower volatility on low-GCS tasks and slightly higher volatility on high-GCS Synapse, consistent with the inductive bias interpretation.}
  \label{fig:generalization}
\end{figure*}

The results reveal a consistent, task-dependent pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:generalization}). On low-GCS benchmarks, ApproxDA-TransUNet is dramatically more stable: 18$\times$ lower volatility on Kvasir-SEG (0.042 vs.\ 0.769) and 9$\times$ lower on ISIC~2018 (0.053 vs.\ 0.479). On high-GCS Synapse, this advantage \emph{reverses}: ApproxDA-TransUNet is 1.3$\times$ more volatile than DA-TransUNet (0.542 vs.\ 0.409).

The sign reversal is the central finding. It is not explained by accuracy differences alone---ApproxDA achieves higher test DSC on all three tasks. The reversal suggests a mechanistic interpretation: windowed locality acts as a \emph{stabilizing inductive prior} when it matches the task's intrinsic context scale. On low-GCS tasks, local boundary cues suffice and windowed attention suppresses spurious long-range gradient signals, yielding smooth late-stage optimization. On high-GCS Synapse, the same locality constraint conflicts with the model's need to aggregate cross-organ context, producing noisier gradients and higher volatility.

Importantly, neither model overfits in the conventional sense: all six val--test generalization gaps are $\leq$0.72\%, and test DSC consistently meets or exceeds best validation DSC. The volatility difference is therefore a property of the optimization landscape, not a train--test distribution mismatch.

A comprehensive volatility sweep across all Synapse ApproxDA configurations confirms the pattern is robust: the gate=pam family at $M{\in}\{7,28,112\}$ consistently shows 1.1--1.3$\times$ higher volatility than DA-TransUNet on Synapse, with gate=cam as the sole exception (0.44$\times$---consistent with channel-wise attention being less sensitive to spatial window size).

These results extend the GCS framing from a performance metric to an optimization phenomenon: locality prior alignment with task structure predicts both final accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:obs2}) and training stability. Low-GCS tasks benefit doubly---from inductive bias alignment and from the implicit regularization effect of windowed attention.
