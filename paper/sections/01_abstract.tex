\begin{abstract}
Although attention approximation has become a standard design strategy for efficient vision transformers, it remains unclear when and why approximation improves segmentation accuracy rather than merely reducing computational cost. We introduce \textbf{Global Context Sensitivity (GCS)}, a task-level concept capturing a task's dependence on large-scale spatial context, measurable via window-ablation DSC range ($\Delta$DSC), and hypothesize that it governs approximation sensitivity: empirically high-GCS tasks require careful window tuning to achieve gains; empirically low-GCS tasks are window-robust. To validate this, we design \textbf{ApproxDA-TransUNet}, a controllable dual-attention architecture with three orthogonal approximation operators---windowed PAM, low-rank projection, and grouped CAM---that serves as both a practical efficient model and an interpretable experimental platform. Experiments on three benchmarks confirm the GCS hypothesis: with task-appropriate window selection, ApproxDA-TransUNet achieves 80.94\% DSC on Synapse ($+$1.14\% vs.\ DA-TransUNet, $\Delta\text{DSC}{=}2.30$\,pp), 90.17\% on Kvasir-SEG ($+$1.73\%, $\Delta\text{DSC}{=}0.64$\,pp), and 89.58\% on ISIC 2018 ($+$1.15\%)---a 3.6$\times$ sensitivity ratio confirming GCS as a predictor of window-tuning importance. A secondary finding reveals gradient symmetry collapse: the learnable gate converges to a near-constant $g{\approx}0.5$ regardless of window size or task---a structural limitation of symmetric dual-branch gating. Together, these results shift the research question from \emph{how to approximate} to \emph{when, and at what scale}, with GCS providing a practical task-level concept for guiding this choice.
\end{abstract}
