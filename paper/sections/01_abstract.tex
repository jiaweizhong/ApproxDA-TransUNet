\begin{abstract}
Although attention approximation has become a standard design strategy for efficient vision transformers, it remains unclear when and why approximation improves segmentation accuracy rather than merely reducing computational cost. We introduce \textbf{Context Sensitivity (CS)}, a task-level property measuring how much a task's segmentation performance changes with the spatial receptive field of attention, operationalized as the DSC range across a window size ablation. We hypothesize that CS governs sensitivity to approximation scale: empirically high-CS tasks require careful window tuning to achieve gains; empirically low-CS tasks are window-robust. To validate this, we design \textbf{ApproxDA-TransUNet}, a controllable dual-attention architecture with three orthogonal approximation operators---windowed PAM (spatial scope $M$), low-rank projection (representation fidelity $r$), and grouped CAM (channel scope $G$)---that serves as both a practical efficient model and an interpretable experimental platform, reducing per-GPU training memory from 11.5\,GB to 6.4\,GB and enabling multi-GPU distributed training. Experiments on three benchmarks confirm the CS hypothesis: with task-appropriate window selection, ApproxDA-TransUNet improves multi-organ CT segmentation ($+$1.14\% vs.\ DA-TransUNet on Synapse with $M{=}28$; 80.94\% DSC, CS=2.30), polyp segmentation ($+$1.73\% on Kvasir-SEG with $M{=}56$; 90.17\% DSC, CS=0.64), and skin lesion segmentation ($+$1.15\% on ISIC 2018 with $M{=}7$; 89.58\% DSC)---a 3.6$\times$ sensitivity ratio on the tasks with full ablations confirming CS as a predictor of window-tuning importance. The DSC-vs-$M$ curve on Synapse is non-monotonic (peak at $M{=}28$), directly paralleling the bias-variance tradeoff: under-context windows have high bias; over-context windows introduce spurious variance; the intermediate optimum balances both. A secondary finding reveals gradient symmetry collapse: learnable dual-branch gating converges to a near-constant $g{\approx}0.5$ regardless of window size, confirmed at both $M{=}7$ and $M{=}14$. These results shift the research question from \emph{how to approximate} to \emph{when, and at what scale}, with CS providing a practical task-level criterion.
\end{abstract}
