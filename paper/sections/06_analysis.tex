\subsection{Observation 1: Gate Collapse}
\label{sec:collapse}

\textbf{Derivation.} Given fused output $\mathbf{F} = g\mathbf{F}_\text{P} + (1-g)\mathbf{F}_\text{C}$, the gate gradient is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial g} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{F}} \cdot \left(\mathbf{F}_\text{P} - \mathbf{F}_\text{C}\right).
  \label{eq:gate_grad}
\end{equation}
At initialization $g{\approx}0.5$, both branches receive equal gradient $\tfrac{1}{2}\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{F}$. Without incentive to differentiate, $\mathbf{F}_\text{P} \approx \mathbf{F}_\text{C}$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate_grad}$\approx \mathbf{0}$. This is self-reinforcing: equal gradients $\to$ convergent representations $\to$ near-zero gate gradient $\to$ $g$ fixed at~0.5. This represents a \textbf{stable equilibrium of symmetric dual-branch optimization}: once reached, no first-order gradient signal can escape it.

\textbf{Empirical confirmation.} After 300 epochs, the gate range at $M{=}7$ is $[0.4993,\,0.5010]$ with $\Delta g{=}0.0017$. At $M{=}14$: $g{\approx}0.5002$ with $\Delta g{\approx}0.0000$. Collapse is identical at both window sizes, confirming it is a fundamental property of symmetric two-branch gating and not a consequence of limited receptive field. Figure~\ref{fig:gate_collapse} visualizes the collapse trajectory.

\textbf{Design implication.} Symmetric two-branch gating is an unstable fixed point that any dual-attention architecture will converge to without explicit symmetry breaking. Future designs should initialize branches asymmetrically, use diversity-encouraging loss terms, or replace the gate with a non-symmetric routing mechanism to escape this degenerate equilibrium.

\subsection{Observation 2: Task-Dependent Approximation Effectiveness}

Although ApproxDA adopts the same approximation operators across all experiments, its effectiveness varies considerably among different segmentation datasets. ApproxDA consistently underperforms the original dual-attention module on Synapse, while achieving competitive or improved performance on Kvasir.

We attribute this phenomenon to the different global contextual requirements of the underlying segmentation tasks. Multi-organ abdominal CT segmentation requires reasoning over long-range anatomical relationships among multiple organs. Aggressive attention approximation inevitably weakens such global dependency modeling, leading to performance degradation.

In contrast, polyp and skin lesion segmentation are primarily determined by local appearance, boundary contrast, and texture consistency. The performance improvement on Kvasir-SEG should not be interpreted as approximation creating additional discriminative information. Rather, we hypothesize that attention approximation introduces an \emph{inductive bias} favoring local interactions while suppressing unnecessary long-range dependencies. For low-GCR tasks, where segmentation is primarily governed by local boundary appearance and texture, this bias better matches the intrinsic structure of the problem and may simultaneously act as an implicit regularizer, reducing overfitting to spurious long-range correlations. This parallels the known effectiveness of local-receptive-field architectures on texture-dominant tasks, where enforcing locality is not a limitation but a correct prior. Validating this inductive bias hypothesis---for example, through attention-distance analysis showing that DA-TransUNet attends to distant irrelevant regions on Kvasir---is an important direction for future work. This interpretation motivates the more precise characterization below.

\subsection{The GCR Hypothesis}

\textbf{Global Context Requirement (GCR)} is a latent task property: the degree to which accurate segmentation depends on long-range spatial dependencies between distant anatomical elements. High-GCR tasks (multi-organ CT) require cross-organ anatomical reasoning---liver position relative to spleen, aorta relative to vena cava---where disrupting global spatial interaction degrades delineation. Low-GCR tasks (polyp, skin lesion) are dominated by local boundary contrast and texture, with minimal dependence on distant context.

We propose that GCR governs the direction and magnitude of approximation's effect, which we treat as a predictive claim rather than a proven causal law:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_\text{DSC} \approx f(\text{GCR}), \quad f'(\cdot) < 0,
  \label{eq:gcr_hypothesis}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_\text{DSC}$ = DSC(ApproxDA) $-$ DSC(DA-TransUNet). Equation~\eqref{eq:gcr_hypothesis} is a conceptual claim, not an analytic proof. It summarizes the empirical pattern: the same ApproxDA configuration achieves $\Delta_\text{DSC}{=}{+}0.80\%$ on low-GCR Kvasir-SEG and $\Delta_\text{DSC}{=}{-}1.16\%$ on high-GCR Synapse---opposite signs consistent with a monotonically decreasing relationship. Figure~\ref{fig:gcr_curve} illustrates this GCR-governed pattern with the two confirmed benchmarks. A natural experimental proxy for GCR is \emph{window sensitivity}: the slope of DSC as attention window $M$ decreases. Because larger $M$ captures more global context, a steep drop as $M$ shrinks signals high GCR, while a near-flat curve indicates low GCR---consistent with the window ablation in Table~\ref{tab:window_ablation}. We treat systematic GCR estimation as a direction for future work.

Attention approximation should not be regarded as a binary design choice but as a configurable inductive bias whose appropriateness depends on GCR. Different approximation strengths suit different GCR regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:approx_space}). That the same collapsed gate=learn routing helps on low-GCR Kvasir ($+$0.80\%) but hurts on high-GCR Synapse further underscores this task-dependence: the fixed equilibrium is not inherently harmful---whether it helps or hurts is determined by whether the task demands global reasoning.

\subsection{Design Principles}

The GCR framework and the gate collapse finding together provide three practical principles for efficient attention design in medical image segmentation.

First, attention approximation should be selected according to the contextual characteristics of the target segmentation task, rather than applied uniformly across all medical imaging applications.

Second, adaptive routing mechanisms require sufficient feature diversity between attention branches; otherwise, gradient symmetry may cause routing collapse regardless of window size or rank.

Finally, future efficient attention architectures should explicitly consider when approximation is appropriate---not just how to make it efficient---rather than focusing exclusively on reducing theoretical complexity. Figure~\ref{fig:guideline} summarizes these guidelines as a practical decision tree.

\subsection{Limitations}

GCR is currently characterized qualitatively rather than quantitatively. Although window sensitivity provides a practical proxy, a rigorous GCR estimator---one that generalizes across imaging modalities, organs, and dataset sizes---remains to be developed. A natural candidate proxy, center-cropping the input to restrict visible context, is valid only when target structures occupy a consistent image location. For tasks such as polyp or skin lesion segmentation, where targets appear at arbitrary positions, small center crops exclude the target entirely, causing DSC to collapse regardless of the model's actual context dependence. This means center-crop sensitivity cannot serve as a GCR probe for randomly-positioned targets; window sensitivity within the model is the appropriate substitute. Additionally, the cross-task comparison is limited to two benchmarks (Synapse and Kvasir-SEG); the GCR hypothesis should be validated on a broader range of medical segmentation tasks before being applied prescriptively. Finally, the inductive bias benefit observed on low-GCR tasks is an empirical observation; its theoretical basis and the conditions under which it holds warrant further investigation.
