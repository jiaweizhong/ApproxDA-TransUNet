\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented ApproxDA-TransUNet, a controllable approximation framework derived from the dual-attention module of DA-TransUNet~\cite{sun2024datransunet}. Rather than proposing another efficient attention operator, ApproxDA-TransUNet serves as a scientific instrument for investigating how approximation strength interacts with different medical image segmentation tasks.

Our results indicate that sensitivity to approximation scale is associated with task \emph{Context Sensitivity (CS)}: the degree to which performance changes with the attention receptive field. With appropriate window selection, ApproxDA-TransUNet outperforms DA-TransUNet on all three benchmarks: $+$1.14\% DSC on Synapse multi-organ CT (80.94\%, $M{=}28$), $+$1.73\% DSC on Kvasir-SEG polyp segmentation (90.17\%, $M{=}56$), and $+$1.15\% DSC on ISIC 2018 skin lesion segmentation (89.58\%, $M{=}7$). The 3.6$\times$ sensitivity ratio (CS=2.30 pp vs.\ 0.64 pp) confirms that empirically high-CS tasks require careful window tuning while empirically low-CS tasks are window-robust. The non-monotonic Synapse DSC-vs-$M$ curve---peak at intermediate $M{=}28$, declining at both extremes---is analogous to the bias-variance tradeoff: under-context windows have high bias; over-context windows introduce spurious variance.

Furthermore, we identify two evidence-supported mechanisms suggesting why approximation improves performance: inductive bias alignment (locality prior matching task structure) and context interference reduction (limiting spurious distant correlations at intermediate window scales). Attention-map evidence directly supports the first: windowed PAM concentrates 62.3\% of its effect on polyp regions vs.\ 34.2\% for global attention (1.82$\times$ ratio).

Finally, our analysis reveals that learnable dual-branch routing collapses to $g{\approx}0.5$ regardless of window size or rank---a fundamental limitation of symmetric two-branch gating with broad implications for routing mechanism design.

Together, these findings shift the research question from \emph{how to approximate} to \emph{when, and at what scale}, with Context Sensitivity providing a practical task-level criterion. Rather than asking \emph{how} attention should be approximated, future research may benefit from first asking \emph{how much context} a task actually requires---with Context Sensitivity providing a practical, ablation-grounded criterion for that question.
